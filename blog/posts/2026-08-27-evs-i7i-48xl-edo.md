Two launches landed around Amazon EVS's first birthday: a bigger i7i host, and a tool that deploys a full VCF 9 environment from one CloudFormation stack.

**i7i.metal-48xl.** It's double the 24xl: 96 physical cores on 5th Gen Intel Xeon, 1,536 GiB of memory, twelve 3.75 TB NVMe drives and 100 Gbps of networking. AWS says i7i gives up to 23% better compute and more than 10% better price performance than i4i. A bigger host means fewer hosts for the same work, and with EVS capped at 32 hosts per environment, more capacity fits in one environment. It also leaves more NVMe per host, which helps when you want memory tiering and still need vSAN capacity.

**EVS Deployment Orchestrator (EDO).** With VCF 9, EVS provisions the hosts and you install VCF yourself. EDO automates both. You write one YAML blueprint, upload the VCF installer OVA and ovftool to S3, store your Broadcom depot token in Secrets Manager, and launch a CloudFormation stack. A small runner instance then works through nine stages:
1. **AWS networking** (5 to 10 minutes): Route 53 zones and records, the Resolver endpoint, DHCP options, NAT and VPC Route Server with BGP peers.
2. **Hosts** (45 to 100 minutes): creates the EVS environment and the bare-metal hosts.
3. **VCF** (3 to 5.5 hours): deploys the VCF Installer on a temporary datastore, syncs the depot and runs bring-up. That gives you vCenter, vSAN, NSX Manager, SDDC Manager, VCF Operations and an NSX Edge cluster connected to your VPC.

A full build takes about four to seven hours, unattended, and every stage checkpoints, so a failure resumes from where it stopped.

If you need to build EVS environments more than once, for test, DR or several business units, EDO turns a multi-day manual task into an overnight job. Run its quota check first: bare-metal hosts often hit EC2 vCPU quotas.
